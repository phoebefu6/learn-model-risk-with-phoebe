What model risk actually is

Brackwater, a consumer lender, submitted its new credit scorecard with a development AUC of 0.863 and a recommendation to deploy. The number was correct. It reproduced every time the query ran. Eleven months later the same model, scored on the following year's applications, discriminates at 0.682 - and a benchmark built from three columns anybody could have written scores 0.676. Nothing in that story is a modelling error. It is a model risk story, which is a different thing, and it is the thing the person who signs the approval is accountable for.

LiveModel risk is not the model being wrong4 min▶

The intuitive definition is that model risk is the risk the model is inaccurate. That definition is wrong in a way that costs money, because it points every review at the one question the modellers have already spent months on. They know the accuracy. What nobody has examined is everything around it.

The supervisory definition is about consequences rather than correctness: model risk is the potential for adverse financial consequences arising from decisions made on the back of model output. Read that carefully and the surface area gets much larger than a performance metric. A model that is accurate on the population it was built for and is then pointed at a different population is a model risk event. A model whose number is correct but is read as a certainty when it was built as an estimate is a model risk event. The guidance is explicit that a fundamentally sound model producing accurate outputs consistent with its design objective can still exhibit high model risk, if it is misapplied or misused.

Real world

Brackwater's scorecard was built on applications from one year and deployed against the next, during which the share of thin-file applicants in the book went from 19.0 percent to 34.0 percent. No line of code changed and nobody made a mistake. The model was simply answering a question about a population that had stopped existing, and the cutoff that approved 75.0 percent of applicants on the development sample approved 69.3 percent of the new one, with the bad rate among those approved rising from 19.5 percent to 24.7 percent.

Self-studyThe words that will keep coming back4 min▶
  • Model. In the current guidance, a simplified representation of real-world relationships among observed characteristics, values and events. Deliberately broad: a spreadsheet with an assumption in it qualifies.
  • Inherent risk. What the model is like before anyone uses it: its assumptions, its complexity, the quality of its inputs, the constraints in its data.
  • Exposure. How much rides on the output. Bigger portfolio, bigger exposure.
  • Purpose. What the output is for. A model used to meet a regulatory requirement or manage financial risk carries more weight than one that sizes a marketing list.
  • Materiality. Purpose and exposure together. This is the dial that decides how much review a model earns.
  • Effective challenge. Critical analysis by objective experts who can actually make the change happen. Session 2 is entirely about the last clause.
  • Validation. The work of deciding whether the model performs as expected, what its limitations are, and whether the evidence offered actually supports the claim being made.
  • Aggregate risk. What happens when twenty models lean on the same assumption, the same feed, or the same vendor. Each one looks fine alone.
Part 1 · covers the four factors and how they combine

Four things set the risk, and only one is about accuracy

10 min live

The current guidance decomposes model risk into inherent risk, exposure, purpose and use. The first is a property of the model. The middle two are properties of the decision it feeds, and together they are called materiality. The fourth is a property of the people, and it is the one that turns a good model into a bad outcome without anybody touching the code.

LiveWhy materiality is the only sustainable way to allocate review4 min▶

Every institution that tries to validate everything to the same depth ends up validating nothing well, because the queue grows faster than the team. The guidance solves this by saying the rigour should be commensurate with the risk, and by giving materiality as the dial: a model may be judged immaterial on the basis of its exposure and purpose, in which case the sensible treatment is to record that it exists and watch for the conditions under which it would stop being immaterial.

That last clause is the part most registers get wrong. An immaterial model is not an unwatched one. The pricing sheet nobody validated because it only covered one product line becomes material the quarter that product line triples, and the trigger for a fresh look should have been written down when the model was first tiered, not improvised afterwards.

Self-studyAggregate risk, and why twenty green models can still be a problem3 min▶

Model risk is assessed both for individual models and in aggregate, and the aggregate view asks a question no single validation can: what do these models share. Interactions and dependencies between them, reliance on common assumptions, a common data feed, a common methodology - any of these can move a whole portfolio of models in the same direction at the same time.

The practical version for a leader: ask what happens to the other models if the assumption in front of you turns out to be wrong. If the answer is "nothing, they are independent", ask who verified that. Shared inputs are the usual culprit, and they rarely appear in any individual model document.

Part 2 · covers the April 2026 guidance and what it changed

The text everybody quotes was replaced this year

8 min live

For fifteen years the reference for this discipline was SR 11-7, issued by the Federal Reserve and the OCC in April 2011. On 17 April 2026 the agencies - now including the FDIC - issued revised guidance that supersedes and replaces it, along with the 2021 statement on model risk for anti-money-laundering systems. Most of the framework survived. Four things moved, and one of them changes an argument that has run in every institution for a decade.

LiveThe independence change, and why both readings are defensible5 min▶

The 2011 text said validation involves a degree of independence from development and use, and that it is generally done by staff who are not responsible for either and have no stake in the outcome. That sentence built a lot of org charts. The 2026 text says something different: that the quality of the validation process depends on the rigour and effectiveness of the review rather than on the organisational structure of the risk management function.

There is a real argument on both sides and it is worth having out loud rather than settling by preference. The case for the change is that separation was never sufficient: a separate team with no domain knowledge and no standing produces a document, not a challenge, and plenty of institutions have built exactly that. The case against is that structure is the only part of this that survives a change of personnel. Rigour depends on who is in the chair; a reporting line does not.

What the 2026 text does not do is remove independence from the definition of effective challenge, where it still sits alongside expertise and standing. The most defensible reading is that structure stopped being the test and did not stop being evidence. If your validators report to the head of modelling, you now have to demonstrate the rigour some other way, and the burden sits with you.

Real world

Brackwater's validation sat with a two-person team inside the analytics function, reporting to the same director as the model developers. Nobody in that team lacked expertise. What they lacked was the standing to stop a launch that the commercial plan had already been written around, and the finding they did raise about the collections field was recorded as an observation rather than a condition.

Self-studyWhere the generative AI line leaves you3 min▶

The 2026 guidance puts generative and agentic AI outside its scope explicitly, on the grounds that they are novel and rapidly evolving, while stating that its principles do apply to traditional statistical and quantitative models and to non-generative, non-agentic AI. It also says that a banking organisation's own risk management and governance practices should guide what controls apply to anything the document does not cover.

Read plainly, that is a gap with your name on it. The model risk regime will not tell you how to govern a large language model in a customer-facing process; the AI-specific regimes will, and learn-ai-governance-with-phoebe covers those - the EU AI Act tiers and obligations, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, and the MAS expectations for Singapore. The practical split is the one the guidance itself draws: model risk practice governs the statistical and non-generative models, and the AI-specific regimes pick up where it stops.

Part 3 · build-along

Score a model you already own

20 min build-along

No laptop. Pick one model your area depends on - a scorecard, a forecast, a pricing sheet, an allocation rule, anything whose output somebody acts on - and put it through the four factors out loud. The point is not to produce a number. It is to find out which of the four you cannot answer, because that is the one that will hurt.

Name the decision, not the model. Say out loud what happens differently because this output exists. If nobody can name an action that changes, the exposure is zero and you have found a model to retire rather than validate.

Size the exposure. What volume, what value, how many customers, over what period. This is the one factor that is genuinely quantitative, so refuse adjectives here.

State the purpose honestly. Is the output meeting a regulatory requirement, managing financial risk, or informing a commercial choice. The first two carry more weight, and people routinely understate this to avoid the review that follows.

Ask what the model assumes that nobody has checked lately. Not the methodology - the assumptions. Which relationship is presumed stable, which population is presumed unchanged, which input is presumed to mean today what it meant when the model was built.

Ask how it is being used now versus how it was built to be used. This is the factor that produces the most uncomfortable answers, because scope creep in model use leaves no trace in any document. Brackwater's scorecard was built to rank applicants and by year two was setting prices.

Write one sentence. "This model is [material / not material] because [exposure] and [purpose], and the thing most likely to go wrong is [assumption or use]." If you cannot finish it, that is tonight's finding.

The question that ends the meeting fastest. Ask what would have to be true for this model to be wrong in a way nobody would notice for six months. The good answers are specific - a feed that silently changes definition, a population that drifts, a threshold that was tuned once - and each of them is a monitoring requirement you can write down before the model goes live.

Check yourself

Three questions

A pricing model is accurate, well documented, and validated. The business then starts using it to set limits for a customer segment it was never built for. Under the current guidance, what has happened to its model risk?

The guidance states directly that a fundamentally sound model producing accurate outputs consistent with its design objective can exhibit high model risk if it is misapplied or misused. Use is a factor in its own right, and it is the one that moves without anybody editing the model.

Your institution deems a model immaterial on the basis of its small portfolio and commercial purpose. What is the sound treatment?

Immaterial does not mean unwatched. The guidance describes identifying those models and monitoring performance and the conditions under which their use may become material in future. The trigger belongs in writing at the point of tiering.

Brackwater's validators sit inside the analytics function and report to the same director as the developers. Under the April 2026 guidance, is that acceptable?

The text moved the test from structure to rigour without removing independence from the definition of effective challenge, where it sits alongside expertise and standing. Structure stopped being the test; it did not stop being evidence. Brackwater's team had the expertise and lacked the standing, which is the failure mode the definition is pointing at.

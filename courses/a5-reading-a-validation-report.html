Learn Model Risk with Phoebe · Leader session 5 of 6

A condition is a control, or it is a sentence in a document

Brackwater's validation came back with four findings and a recommendation to approve with conditions. On this evidence that is the right recommendation. Whether it is worth anything depends entirely on what the conditions section says, and most conditions sections are full of sentences: the owner should review, the team will consider, performance will be monitored closely. Nobody is named, nothing is dated, and nothing happens if none of it is done. A model approved on those terms has been approved outright, with a paragraph attached that makes everybody feel better. Tonight is how to read the document you are being asked to act on, what severity is actually grading, and the three parts a condition needs before it changes anybody's behaviour.

LiveA validation report is an argument, not a summary4 min▶

The most useful habit you can bring to one of these documents is to stop reading it as a report on a model and start reading it as an argument about a claim. Somebody asserted something, in writing, in the model document. Validation evaluates whether the model performs as expected, assesses its reliability and its limitations, and clarifies appropriate use and whether corrective actions may be warranted. Everything in the report exists to support or undermine that assertion, and a report that never states the assertion is asking you to grade an answer without showing you the question.

This matters because of what it does to the reading order. If the claim is stated up front, every finding underneath it has an obvious weight: it either survives the claim, narrows it, or breaks it. If the claim is missing, the findings become a list of technical observations of unknown importance, and the only person in the room who can weigh them is the person who wrote them. That is how a report ends up being summarised to a committee by its own author, which is a governance failure with a perfectly pleasant surface.

Real world

Brackwater's model document made one claim: an eleven-feature scorecard that discriminates at 0.863 on the development sample, recommended for deployment. That sentence is the thing being validated. Not the model, not the team, not the method. By the end of the report, three of the four findings are about that sentence rather than about the scorecard, and the fourth is about the world the scorecard was deployed into.

Self-studyThe words this session runs on3 min▶
  • The claim. What the model document asserts, in one sentence. The thing the report is about.
  • Finding. A statement of what the evidence showed, written so that the model owner can agree with the fact even while disagreeing with the grade.
  • Severity. The weight attached to a finding. A judgement about consequence, made by the validator and signed off by somebody, and the field most often quietly edited.
  • Observation. A finding that has been graded as not worth a condition. Legitimate, and also the drawer that findings disappear into.
  • Condition. Something that must happen before or during use. A control if it names an owner, a trigger or a date, and a consequence. Otherwise a sentence.
  • Limitation. A boundary on the claim that no amount of remediation removes. It belongs in the report and then in the use.
  • Scope. What was tested, and by omission what was not. An untested area is not a passed one.
  • Recommendation. One of three answers: approve, approve with conditions, or decline to approve.
Part 1 · covers the anatomy of a report, and what severity grades

Five parts, and the shape that tells you the report is honest

10 min live

There is no mandated template for a validation report, and the good ones differ. What the good ones share is that a reader who has never seen the model can find five things quickly: what was claimed, what was found, how heavily each finding weighs, what has to happen as a result, and what the answer is. Read the five in order and the document either holds together or visibly does not.

LiveOrganised by component, or organised by whatever got run4 min▶

Session 3 gave the three components of validation in the order the guidance uses them: conceptual soundness, outcomes analysis, and ongoing model monitoring. A report organised around those three headings is one a non-specialist can follow, for a reason that has nothing to do with taste. The three headings exist independently of what anybody ran. If the outcomes analysis section is thin, you can see that it is thin. If there is no ongoing monitoring section, the absence is on the page.

A report organised around the work that happened has the opposite property. Its sections are the tests somebody chose to run, so every section is full, every section reports a result, and the document looks complete no matter how much was skipped. There is no empty heading, because the headings were written after the work. You are reading a list of things that were done, presented as an assessment of a model, and the only way to find the gap is to already know what should have been there.

The leader's move is not to redesign anybody's template. It is to ask, for each of the three components, which section covers it and what it concluded. Three questions, and a report that cannot answer one of them has told you what it is.

LiveSeverity grades the consequence, not the cleverness4 min▶

Severity is the field that decides whether anything happens, and it is graded by people who have just spent six weeks on the work. Two failure modes follow from that, and they pull in opposite directions.

Grading by difficulty. A finding that took three weeks of forensic effort to uncover feels heavier than one that was obvious on the first afternoon. It is not. Severity is a statement about what happens if the finding is correct and nothing is done about it, and the effort spent discovering it is information about the review, not about the model. The obvious finding that puts the whole claim in doubt outranks the ingenious one that narrows it slightly.

Grading by embarrassment. The mirror image, and the more common one. A finding is graded down because the consequence of grading it up is a delayed launch, an awkward conversation, or a colleague's reputation. This is rarely done dishonestly. It is done by adding qualifiers until the sentence is true and weightless, and the tell is that the finding text and the severity no longer describe the same event.

The discipline that fixes both is to write the consequence before choosing the grade. One sentence: if this finding is right and nothing changes, the following happens to the following decisions. Grade that sentence. If the sentence is hard to write, the finding is not yet understood well enough to grade, and that is itself worth saying out loud.

Real world

Brackwater's leaked field was not hard to find. Anybody who asked when the collections contact was recorded relative to the outcome window would have had it in an afternoon. It is also the finding that breaks the claim, because the submitted 0.863 depends on it and the honest development figure is 0.772. Session 2 recorded what actually happened: it was written up as an observation rather than a condition, and an observation obliges nobody.

Self-studyThe four Brackwater findings, and what each one weighs5 min▶

Four findings, in the order a reader can follow rather than the order they were discovered. None of them require you to evaluate a metric; each is a statement about whether the submitted evidence supports the submitted claim.

  • A field that could not have been available at decision time. The collections field records contacts logged after the outcome window opens, so it is a consequence of default rather than a predictor of it. On its own it discriminates at 0.748 against the submitted 0.863. Refit without it, development performance falls to 0.772, so the leak was worth 0.091. Consequence if right: the headline number in the model document is not a number the model can reproduce in production. This is the finding that decides the report.
  • Performance on a later vintage. The honest model, scored on the following year's 2,000 applications, discriminates at 0.682, a further drop of 0.090. Consequence if right: the approved performance and the lived performance are different numbers, and the cutoff calibrated on the first is being applied to the second.
  • Calibration that is sound in aggregate and wrong in every band. Out of time, the model predicts a mean default rate of 0.330 against an actual 0.324, a gap of -0.006 and as close as anybody reports. By quintile of predicted risk the picture inverts: the safest fifth is under-predicted by 0.061, and the riskiest fifth is over-predicted by 0.142. Consequence if right: the aggregate figure that was reported would have passed this model, and the decisions are made in the bands.
  • A population that moved. The share of thin-file applicants in the book went from 19.0 percent to 34.0 percent between the two vintages. Consequence if right: the growth is concentrated in the applicants the model has least evidence about, and no monitoring threshold was written that would have fired.

There is a fifth thing in the pack that is not a finding, and it is worth naming as an item in its own right. A three-feature benchmark, built from columns anybody could have written, scores 0.676 out of time against the champion's 0.682. That is not a finding about a defect. It is a finding about what the eleven features, the leak and the validation cycle actually bought, which is 0.006.

Part 2 · covers conditions, and the three available recommendations

What approval with conditions actually buys

8 min live

Approval with conditions is the most common recommendation in this discipline and the least examined. It reads as a compromise between yes and no, which is exactly what it is not. It is a yes, with the use of the model bounded by controls that have to hold. Whether the bounding is real is decided entirely in the wording, and a conditions section can be read in ninety seconds against three tests.

LiveOwner, trigger or date, consequence5 min▶

A condition is a promise that the model's use is bounded until something happens. Three things have to be in the text for that promise to mean anything, and the reason each one is necessary is worth having separately, because conditions usually fail on exactly one of them.

An owner, and a person rather than a role. "The modelling team will refit the scorecard" assigns the work to an abstraction. Teams reorganise, priorities move, and no abstraction has ever taken a phone call. A named individual can be asked in a corridor, can hand the item over explicitly when they move on, and can say no, which is information you want early. The test is whether you could send one email. If you would have to ask who owns this first, the condition has no owner.

A trigger or a date, written now. Time-bound conditions get a date. Event-bound ones get a trigger: when the book crosses a stated size, when the next vintage closes, when the feed definition changes, when the model is put to a new use. Either is acceptable and the mixture is common. What is not acceptable is "as soon as practicable", which is a date chosen later by whoever is least inconvenienced by the delay. A trigger written in advance also has a property a date does not: it survives the plan changing, because it describes the world rather than the calendar.

A consequence if it is not met, decided before it is needed. This is the part that is almost always missing, and it is the part that makes the other two load-bearing. If the condition is not met by the date, what happens: does the model stop, does a use narrow, does the approval lapse, does it escalate to a named committee. Decide it now, while the model is not yet in production and nobody is under pressure. Decided later, in the week the date is missed, the answer is always an extension, because by then the model is running the book and stopping it is a business event.

Two smaller tests catch most of what survives those three. Is the condition observable by somebody who is not thinking about model risk that day, and is it recorded somewhere other than the report. Documentation supports the tracking of recommendations, responses and exceptions, and a condition that lives only in a PDF in a shared drive is not being tracked, whatever the policy says. Session 4 made the same point about the register: the artefact that holds the item is the one that decides whether the item exists.

Real world

A conditions section for Brackwater that would actually bound the use has four entries, and each one names a person, a date and a consequence. Refit without collections and report the honest development and out-of-time figures by a stated date, or the score may not be used to set price. Report calibration by band, not in aggregate, in every monthly pack, or the monthly pack is returned unaccepted. Set and publish a thin-file share threshold, with the action that fires when it is crossed. And re-examine the eleven-feature design against the three-feature benchmark at the next scheduled review, given that out of time the difference between them is 0.006.

LiveThe three answers, and which one this evidence supports4 min▶

Approve. The evidence supports the claim, the limitations are stated, and the use described in the model document is the use being approved. This is not a rare answer and it should not be treated as suspicious. A well-built model with honest evidence and a clear statement of what it cannot do earns a clean approval, and a function that never gives one is teaching its model owners that the report is theatre.

Approve with conditions. The model can be used, and the use is bounded until stated things happen. This is the answer Brackwater's evidence supports, and it is worth being precise about why. Nothing in the four findings says the scorecard cannot rank applicants. What they say is that the number in the document is not the number the model produces honestly, that the later vintage performs worse than the approved figure, that the aggregate calibration conceals the band-level picture, and that the population has moved toward the applicants the model knows least about. Every one of those is addressable, and every one of them is a boundary on use until it is addressed. That combination is the definition of this recommendation.

Decline to approve. Reserved for a model that cannot be used safely at all in the use proposed. Not a model that disappoints, not a model whose author was careless, and not a model whose findings are numerous. The test is whether a bounded use exists: if you can describe a narrower use, a lower limit, or a closer monitoring regime under which the model is defensible, the answer is conditions, and the narrowing belongs in the conditions. If no such setting exists, conditions are dishonest, because you would be writing controls around a use that should not happen. Session 6 is about that answer, including how to give it without leaving the business stranded.

One structural point holds across all three. The recommendation is the last thing in the report and the first thing everybody reads, which means it routinely gets negotiated independently of the findings above it. When a recommendation is upgraded, the findings almost never change to match, and the document is left arguing with itself. A report whose recommendation does not follow from its own severity grades has recorded that a decision was made somewhere else, and the useful question is where.

Self-studyThe claim not to make, and the risk that stays after you sign4 min▶

There is a sentence everybody in the room reaches for once the findings are on the table, and it is wrong. It is some version of "the champion is worthless, the challenger is better, build the simple one." On this evidence that claim cannot be sustained. Out of time the champion scores 0.682 and the three-feature challenger 0.676, which is a difference of 0.006 and well inside the noise of a 2,000-application sample. At their respective cutoffs they win on different axes: the champion approves 69.3 percent of applicants with 24.7 percent bad among those approved, the challenger approves 72.2 percent with 25.3 percent bad. The champion buys 0.6 points of bad rate and gives up 2.9 points of volume. Which of those a lender prefers is a business question, not a validation finding.

The defensible finding is narrower and sharper: the evidence submitted did not support the claim made. That is a statement about the model document, not about the model, and it is the one you can hold in front of a supervisor, a committee or the model owner without retreating. It also survives the most likely counterattack, which is a rebuttal about the challenger's own weaknesses, because the finding was never about the challenger.

Notice what the narrower claim does to the conditions. If the finding is "the model is bad", the only condition available is "build a new one", which is not a control and will not happen on any timescale you can name. If the finding is "the evidence did not support the claim", the conditions write themselves: produce honest evidence for the claim you want to make, or make a smaller claim. That is achievable, datable and ownable, which is the whole point.

The last thing to carry out of the report is what it does not remove. Even with sound modelling practices and rigorous validation, material model risk can remain. That is a statement in the guidance, not a pessimistic aside, and it puts a short list of obligations on the people who use the output rather than on the people who validated it: understand and communicate the model's limitations, monitor performance, periodically review whether the model is still relevant, and supplement the output with complementary analysis and information. Session 3 covered that list from the validator's side. Tonight it arrives as the thing a signature does not buy: the conditions bound the use, the report bounds the claim, and the residual risk stays with the business.

Part 3 · build-along

Re-read a report you already approved

20 min build-along

No laptop. Bring the last validation report your area signed, ideally one that was approved with conditions, and work the seven steps out loud. The output is not a re-opened decision. It is a list of the conditions that turned out to be sentences, and one rewritten condition that would have held.

Find the claim, and read it aloud. One sentence from the model document saying what this model does and how well. If the report does not contain it, write what you believe it was, and notice that you are now reconstructing the question after grading the answer.

List the findings without their severities. Just the facts, in the order a stranger could follow. Cover them up before you look at the grades, because the grades will otherwise tell you what to think about each one.

Grade each one yourself, by consequence. For each finding, one sentence: if this is right and nothing changes, the following happens to the following decision. Then compare your grade with the one in the report and look only at the disagreements.

Ask what is not in the report. Which of the three components got the least space, what was out of scope, and whether the scope was recorded anywhere. An untested area that nobody wrote down as untested will be read next year as an area that passed.

Run the three tests on every condition. A named person, a date or a trigger, a stated consequence. Count how many of the conditions have all three. In most packs the number is zero or one, and saying the count out loud is the most useful thing that will happen in this session.

Find out what actually happened to each condition. Met, missed, extended, or quietly dropped. Then ask who would have noticed a miss, and whether anybody did. This is the step that tells you whether your conditions are a control system or a writing style.

Rewrite one condition so it would have held. Take the weakest one and put a name, a date or a trigger, and a consequence into it. Read it back and ask whether you would have been willing to enforce the consequence in the month it fell due. If not, the consequence is wrong and the condition is still a sentence.

The question that tests a conditions section in one move. Pick any condition and ask what would have happened last quarter if it had been missed. Not what the policy says: what would actually have happened, to this model, in this business. If the honest answer is that the date would have moved, you are looking at a target rather than a control, and the model was approved outright.

Check yourself

Three questions

A validation report approves a model subject to one condition: "The model owner will monitor performance closely and report any deterioration to the risk committee." What is wrong with it?

Three parts make a condition a control: a named owner, a trigger or a date, and a stated consequence if it is not met. This one has an activity and a destination but nothing that can be missed, so there is no moment at which anybody is late and nothing narrows when they are. A model approved on this condition has been approved outright.

Two findings sit in front of you. One took a validator three weeks of forensic work to uncover and narrows the model's use slightly. The other was obvious within a day and means the headline performance figure cannot be reproduced in production. How should severity be assigned?

Severity is a statement about what happens if the finding is correct and nothing is done. Discovery effort is information about the review, not about the model. Writing the consequence sentence first and grading that sentence defends against both failure modes: inflating a clever finding, and deflating an awkward one.

Brackwater's champion scores 0.682 out of time against a three-feature challenger's 0.676. At their cutoffs the champion approves 69.3 percent with 24.7 percent bad and the challenger 72.2 percent with 25.3 percent bad. What is the defensible finding?

The two models are within 0.006 on discrimination and each wins on a different business axis: the champion buys 0.6 points of bad rate and gives up 2.9 points of volume. Neither "worthless" nor "vindicated" survives that. What does survive is that the submitted 0.863 depended on a field that could not have been available at decision time, so the evidence offered did not support the claim made, and that finding writes its own conditions.
